In the modern world, it is necessary for many businesses and organisations to collect user data in order to operate. However, the volume and sensitivity of this data may vary depending on what the specific data is. “Data fuels innovation; therefore, it is critical to have a framework that focuses on regulating how organizations obtain, process, and use data.” (VCE, 2024) Organisations are legally required to obtain consent from an end user before collecting their data. However, the ethics behind what data must be collected and what data must remain private are not always clear.  

  

There are instances where the collection of private data could be considered as justified. For example, law enforcement may require sensitive data regarding an individual in order to stop or investigate crimes (Santa Clara University, 2013). Personal Data is also useful in the Healthcare Sector. "Data collection in healthcare allows health systems to create holistic views of patients, personalize treatments, advance treatment methods, improve communication between doctors and patients, and enhance health outcomes."(Natallia Sakovich, No Date). The collection of certain personal information by healthcare organisations can be used to provide personalised healthcare at a huge scale. 

However, collecting too much of this information makes it easier to exploit or misuse. "Medical data is financially valuable, and your sensitive data may be shared or sold by doctors, hospitals, clinical laboratories, and pharmacies—without your knowledge or consent...In addition to data harms related to exposure and discrimination, the collection of sensitive data by powerful corporations risks the creation of data monopolies that can dominate and condition access to health care."(Carissa Véliz, 2019) 

Balancing this need for information with the rights and privacy of individuals, as well as ensuring this information is handled responsibly, and not used solely for profit or to influence biases, is an incredibly important but also incredibly difficult task. 

  

When accepting cookies on a website, explanations for what the user is consenting to can be long-winded and overcomplicated, accompanied by options like “accept all” and “manage” with no option to reject essential and a lack of clarity around what essential cookies are collecting, making it difficult to understand and leading to uninformed consent (Annmarie Hanlon and Karen Jones, 2023) as it is unrealistic for a user browsing the internet to fully read the terms and conditions for every website they visit. 

Additionally, users are often not aware of what is being done with their data and what the intent behind its collection is, which leads them to be further uninformed. 

Such a case happened with Facebook in 2018, where a political analytics firm had used an external application to pay users to partake in a personality quiz. Unbeknownst to these users, their information and that of themselves was being used to create psychological profiles on users based on their liked posts and other sensitive information, such as their home addresses, and use this information for political campaigns. (University of Greater Manchester, 2021) The end users were giving uninformed consent, leading to their data being used in ways that had neither foreseen nor intended.  

  

For privacy and security to be protected in today’s society, the current laws surrounding privacy protection, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) need to be revised and/or adjusted. Stricter laws must be put in place about exceeding how much of the end user's data is required for the organisation to operate and where this data is being stored and sent. The requirements Organisations should have to meet to be allowed to store and use sensitive information should be more demanding and less indefinite (E.g requiring a Security Certificate) 

It should also be a requirement for Organisations to be more transparent with their cookies, allowing users to reject them in bulk without access to the website being revoked or locked behind a paywall, as is the case for some websites where a monthly or yearly fee must be paid in order for certain cookies to remain disabled. "Consumers seeking to protect their personal data while browsing are confronted with the dilemma: pay up or surrender your privacy through cookie paywalls. Accept or reject and incur a subscription fee to opt out – a decision that could cost up to $75 per year for a single site."(Syrenis, 2024) These are not currently explicitly illegal, but should not be allowed if there is to be protection of the User's privacy in the future. Websites should be required to use a free, clear, and accessible display of the data they collect and allow the user to opt out of as much of this data as possible if they so choose.
